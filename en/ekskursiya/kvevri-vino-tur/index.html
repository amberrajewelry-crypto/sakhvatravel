Georgia invented wine. The qvevri — a clay amphora buried underground — is how Georgians have been fermenting grapes for over 8,000 years. This UNESCO-recognised tradition produces amber wines unlike anything made in European oak barrels. This tour takes you to the source: real family wineries in Kakheti, where the winemaker opens the qvevri and pours straight from a ladle.

Unlike a standard tasting at a commercial cellar with stainless steel tanks, this tour visits small producers making 500–2,000 bottles per year, all sold at the gate. You will not find these wines in any shop.

What Is Qvevri Winemaking?

Grapes are crushed and fermented together with the skins, stems and pips inside the clay qvevri for 5–6 months. No sulphites, no filtration, no additives — only grapes and clay. The resulting wine, called amber or orange wine, has a deep amber colour, rich tannins and a nutty, honeyed complexity that European winemakers are only now beginning to replicate.

Georgia's qvevri winemaking was inscribed on UNESCO's List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2013. The tradition is at least 8,000 years old — older than Ancient Greece, older than Rome. Kakheti, in eastern Georgia, produces over 70% of the country's wine and is the heartland of qvevri culture.

How This Tour Differs from a Standard Tasting

A standard wine tasting in Kakheti means visiting large commercial estates with European equipment — a pleasant experience, but not the real thing. The qvevri tour focuses on family-run maranebi (wine cellars) where the host has inherited both the vessels and the knowledge from his grandfather.

You will see the qvevri buried in the earthen floor of the marani, watch the winemaker open the clay seal, and taste the wine directly from the amphora. The temperature inside is naturally stable at 12–14°C year-round — the original refrigeration system.

Practical Tips

  • Pace yourself. Six to eight wine tastings over a morning adds up. Eat breakfast before departure and drink water between tastings.
  • Wear comfortable shoes. Marani floors can be uneven. Light trainers or walking sandals are fine.
  • Bring cash. Family wineries do not take cards. ATMs are available in the nearest town.
  • Buying wine to take home. Georgian airlines allow wine in checked luggage. Pack bottles in clothing inside a hard-shell suitcase, or ask the winery — they often have bubble wrap.
  • Best season. September and October are harvest months — you may see the rtveli (grape harvest) in progress. Spring (April–May) is the second-best window. Summer is hot but fully operational.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a qvevri?

A qvevri is a clay amphora buried underground, used for fermenting and ageing wine. Georgia's qvevri winemaking tradition is over 8,000 years old and is recognised by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage. The vessels range from 20 litres to 2,000 litres and are lined with beeswax to prevent leakage.

What is amber wine?

Amber wine (also called orange wine) is a white wine made with extended skin contact inside the qvevri, giving it an amber colour and tannins usually found only in red wine. Georgia produces the world's finest amber wines. The flavour profile ranges from honeyed and nutty to deeply tannic depending on the grape variety and skin-contact duration.

Can I buy qvevri wine to take home?

Yes. The family wineries we visit sell their wines on-site, typically ₾15–40 per bottle. They can help you wrap bottles for air travel. Georgian wines exported commercially are far more expensive — buying direct from the producer is always the best value.

How many wines will we taste?

6–8 varieties across 2 wineries, including amber Rkatsiteli, Kakhuri Mtsvane, and red Saperavi made in qvevri. The format is open tasting — you sip at your own pace, not rushed through a fixed programme.

Is this tour suitable for non-drinkers?

Yes. The qvevri craft and the marani itself are fascinating even without alcohol. I can arrange for the winery to prepare grape juice or chacha-free tastings on request — just mention it when booking.
