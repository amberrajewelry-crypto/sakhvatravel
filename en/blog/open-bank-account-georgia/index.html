How to Open a Bank Account in Georgia in 2026: Step-by-Step Guide

Opening a bank account in Georgia as a non-resident is still possible, but since 2023–2024 banks have become stricter: they ask for a connection to the country and sometimes refuse without explanation. Below is a guide that works in 2026: which banks are more open, what documents to bring, and what to do if you're refused.

In short: the two main banks are Bank of Georgia and TBC. You need a passport, sometimes a second document and proof of address. Opening is usually free; it takes from one hour to a few days. If refused, try another branch or bank.

Which banks to choose?

Several banks serve individuals in Georgia, but for a foreigner the two largest are easiest:

  • Bank of Georgia — the largest bank, with a convenient app in English and Russian and multi-currency accounts (GEL/USD/EUR). There's a Solo package for premium clients.
  • TBC Bank — the second largest, also with a strong app and multi-currency support. Many expats open their account here.

There are lesser-known options — Liberty Bank, Credo, ProCredit — but for a non-resident they're less predictable. Start with BoG or TBC.

Both banks issue a multi-currency card (you can hold lari, dollars and euros on one account) and handle SWIFT transfers. That's handy for crypto withdrawals and freelance payments.

What documents do you need?

The basic set for an individual:

  • Passport — mandatory and valid.
  • Second document — sometimes a national ID or driving licence is requested.
  • Proof of address — a rental contract, utility bill or accommodation booking. Not always required, but good to have.
  • Source of income — an employment letter, statement or explanation of where the money comes from (on request).
Compliance note: citizens of some countries may face extra questions or be asked for proof of a residence permit or work in Georgia. It's not a ban, but be ready to explain your connection to the country.

Step-by-step instructions

  1. Choose a branch. Large branches in central Tbilisi are used to foreigners and more often have Russian/English-speaking staff.
  2. Take a ticket for account opening. Say you're a non-resident and want a multi-currency account with a card.
  3. Fill in the form. Provide your address in Georgia and source of income.
  4. Wait for approval. Sometimes the account opens immediately, sometimes the application goes to compliance review (1–5 business days).
  5. Activate the app. Set up online banking, link the card, enable transfers.

Tip: go to a central branch on a weekday morning and say upfront that you're a non-resident. Bring a rental contract — accounts open noticeably more willingly with one.

What if you're refused?

A refusal without a stated reason is possible — it's a compliance decision and isn't always explained. Options:

  • Try another branch of the same bank — decisions are made locally.
  • Go to another bank — if TBC refused, try BoG, and vice versa.
  • Add documents — a residence permit, rental contract or work contract improve your chances.
  • Use a local consultant — relocation firms help open an account for a small fee.

Multi-currency and transfers

Accounts at BoG and TBC are multi-currency: on a single IBAN you hold lari, dollars and euros and switch between them in the app at the market rate. For freelancers this is handy — take payment in USD or EUR and convert when the rate suits you.

An incoming SWIFT transfer usually arrives in 1–3 business days; the fee mostly depends on the sending bank. Wise and Payoneer work with Georgian accounts, which makes withdrawing from foreign platforms easier. For large sums the bank may ask for proof of the source of funds — a standard compliance step.

Mobile banking and cash

The BoG and TBC apps run in English and Russian, with instant transfers by phone number between clients of the same bank. Card, Apple Pay and Google Pay are accepted almost everywhere in cities; in the countryside and at markets you still need cash.

ATMs are plentiful: withdrawing from your own card is free, from another bank's incurs a fee. Some machines accept notes to top up your account. Tip: keep a small stash of lari — marshrutkas, private guides and rural cafés don't always take cards.

Accounts for sole traders and freelancers

Many relocators open not just a personal account but also sole-trader (individual entrepreneur) status. Georgia offers a favourable small-business regime: up to a certain annual turnover the tax is just 1% of income. That is exactly what makes the country popular with freelancers and remote workers — for regular work with foreign clients the legal burden ends up minimal.

The order is: first register the sole trader and small-business status at the House of Justice and the tax office, then open a separate business account at a bank. Returns are filed monthly; at the start it's easier to hire a local accountant — the service is cheap and pays for itself in peace of mind. Keep personal and business spending on different accounts so the turnover stays clear. One thing to grasp: the tax regime and registration are a tax-office procedure, while the bank only opens the account. A single visit won't settle it, but the process is well worn and transparent.

Taxes and currency control for non-residents

Opening an account does not by itself make you a Georgian tax resident or create automatic obligations. Tax residency arises if you spend 183 days or more in the country over 12 months; until then, as an ordinary non-resident client, no income tax is withheld on incoming transfers to your current account.

Georgia has almost no currency restrictions on moving money in or out: you can freely receive transfers from abroad, hold balances in lari, dollars and euros, and convert between them. Banks report large operations under financial-monitoring rules, so for transfers of roughly 30,000 GEL and above you may be asked for documents proving the source of funds — keep a contract, invoice or statement handy.

Interest on personal deposits is not subject to income tax in Georgia, which makes local savings attractive. But remember the rules of your home country: many states require you to declare foreign accounts, and under CRS Georgia exchanges data with dozens of countries. Check your obligations with a tax adviser before parking large sums.

Key takeaways

If you remember just the essentials:

  • Two working banks for a foreigner are Bank of Georgia and TBC; the account is multi-currency: lari, dollars and euros.
  • You need a passport, sometimes a second document and proof of address; opening is usually free.
  • It takes from an hour to five days; a refusal without reason is possible — then try another branch or bank.
  • A rental contract and a residence permit noticeably improve your chances of approval.
  • Freelancers benefit from sole-trader status taxed at 1% — a tax-office procedure, not a bank one.
