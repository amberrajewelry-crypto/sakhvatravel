Georgia is a small country with big mountains — which means fast rivers. Rafting here is open to complete beginners and seasoned paddlers alike: from calm family runs near Tbilisi to roaring rapids in the western gorges. Below — which rivers to raft, how the difficulty grades work, when the high water comes, how much it costs, what gear you need and how to pick a safe operator.

Why rafting in Georgia is worth it

Rafting means floating down a mountain river in an inflatable raft, as a team led by a guide. And Georgia is built for it by geography itself: the Caucasus, melting glaciers, and dozens of rivers carrying cold water through gorges, canyons and forests. The scenery along the banks is a reason to come on its own — sheer cliffs, waterfalls, old towers and bridges that look completely different from the water than from the road.

A key advantage of Georgia specifically is how compact it is. Many rafting rivers sit a couple of hours from Tbilisi or Kutaisi, so you can slot rafting into a trip as a single day without sacrificing the rest of your plans. Rafting in the morning, wine and khachapuri in the afternoon, a warm evening in the city.

And then there's the thrill. Even an easy route delivers a real hit of adrenaline, spray, teamwork on the paddles, and that feeling of triumph when the raft clears a rapid. It's one of those adventures you remember far more vividly than any viewpoint — and it needs no preparation and no gear of your own.

It's also worth noting the value: compared with rafting in the Alps or on the world's headline rivers, a trip in Georgia costs noticeably less for comparable thrills. That makes the country a great place to try rafting for the first time without a pricey tour — and see the Caucasus while you're at it.

Rafting in Georgia is part of a broader "active" scene alongside paragliding, ziplines, canyoning and jeep tours. But rafting stands out because almost anyone can do it: the crew paddles together while the guide handles steering and safety.

Rivers: Aragvi, Kura, Rioni, Tekhuri

Every Georgian river has its own character, season and difficulty. Here are the main options people raft most often.

Aragvi (near Mtskheta)

Probably the most convenient river for anyone based in Tbilisi. The Aragvi flows along the Georgian Military Road and very close to ancient Mtskheta. It offers sections of varying difficulty, and the logistics are simple — you can get there in an hour or so. The ideal way to try rafting for the first time and combine it with Georgia's old capital.

Kura / Mtkvari (Borjomi area)

The country's main river in its upper reaches, around Borjomi and the Borjomi-Kharagauli National Park, offers scenic runs among forests and mineral springs. It's the classic pick for anyone heading toward Borjomi and Bakuriani who wants to add some action to a laid-back resort break.

Rioni and Tekhuri (western Georgia)

More serious rivers run through Imereti and Samegrelo. The Rioni is the largest river in western Georgia, while the Tekhuri with its canyons is prized by lovers of tougher rapids. The base for these runs is usually Kutaisi, which is also handy for the famous canyons nearby. For that neighbouring natural wonder, see our guide to Martvili Canyon.

Racha and Adjara rivers

The mountainous Racha region in the north and the rivers of Adjara in the hills above Batumi are for those who want rafting off the tourist trail, with wild nature and more demanding sections. Here an experienced local operator who knows the water really matters.

River names and sections, and their difficulty grades, depend on the water level in a given season and year. The same river in spring flood and in summer is effectively two different routes. Always confirm the current difficulty and conditions with the operator before you paddle.

Difficulty grades: reading I–VI

Rafting uses the international scale of rapid difficulty, from I to VI. Understanding it helps you pick a route within your ability with a clear head:

GradeWhat it meansWho it suits
I–IICalm water, gentle wavesComplete beginners, families
IIIMedium rapids, spray, manoeuvresBeginners with no fear of water
IVPowerful rapids, teamwork neededFit paddlers with experience
VHard, long, dangerous rapidsExperienced rafters only
VIExtreme, at the limit of runnableProfessionals, rarely commercial

Most commercial trips for tourists in Georgia are grades II–III, sometimes with individual grade-IV sections. Those deliver plenty of adrenaline while staying safe with normal water and a good guide. If you're offered an "easy family run", that's usually grade I–II.

Season: when the water is high

Rafting in Georgia is seasonal, and the time of year directly shapes the character of the run.

  • May–June — high water. Snow melts in the mountains and rivers run full and fast. This is the peak for maximum adrenaline: rapids are stronger, currents faster. But the demands on participants are higher too.
  • July–August — calmer. Water levels drop and currents soften. The best time for beginners, families and a first-ever run: the same routes, run more comfortably.
  • September — the sweet spot. Warm, moderate water, fewer tourists. A lovely option for rafting alongside autumn scenery.

The exact opening and closing of the season shifts from year to year with the weather and water level. In early spring and late autumn the water is cold and high, and commercial trips run less often. When planning rafting, always check current conditions with your operator.

Where to raft: regions and bases

The easiest way to organise rafting is to start from where you're based.

From Tbilisi

The most convenient option is the Aragvi near Mtskheta and along the Georgian Military Road. You can genuinely head out for a run and be back the same day, catching the old capital and its monasteries on the way. A great pick if you're staying in Tbilisi and want an active day without long transfers.

From Kutaisi

Western Georgia means the Rioni, the Tekhuri and proximity to the Martvili and Okatse canyons. Kutaisi is the ideal base for combining rafting with canyon and cave walks. More on western Georgia routes in our article on Kutaisi from Tbilisi.

Borjomi and Bakuriani

People come here for a relaxed resort break, mineral water and mountains — and a run on the Kura adds some action. A good option for a family trip with older children.

How much it costs and what's included

The exact price of rafting depends on the river, route length, season and group size — so the figures below are guidelines, not fixed sums.

Half dayHalf the river + transferGear, guide, sometimes photos
Full dayLong route + lunchAll the above + food, transfer

Operators set the prices and they change by season — confirm the current cost and exact contents before booking.

A standard run almost always includes a wetsuit, helmet, life vest and paddle, a short briefing on the bank and an experienced guide in the raft. Photo and video from the water, hotel transfer and lunch are often offered separately. Ask in advance exactly what's included so you don't overpay on the spot.

Gear and what to bring

Good news: the operator provides the main gear, so you don't need to buy anything. But a few things are worth bringing:

  • A change of clothes and a towel. You'll be soaked after the run — a dry set is essential.
  • Shoes you don't mind ruining. Sandals with a heel strap or old trainers that stay firmly on your feet and don't fear water.
  • Swimwear or something to wear under the wetsuit. Whatever is comfortable and fine to get wet.
  • Sunscreen. You burn faster on the water — the sun reflects off the river.
  • A strap for your glasses. So you don't lose them in the very first rapid.

Phones, watches and anything that fears water are best left in the car or in a waterproof dry bag. A good operator will tell you where to leave your things safely and will offer photos from the water, so you don't have to risk your own gear for the shots.

Safety: the key rules

Rafting is an adventure with a real, not decorative, element of risk. Run well, it's relatively safe — but the rules can't be ignored.

  • Licensed operator only. Check reviews, the state of the gear and whether guides are trained. Cheaper isn't better when it comes to a mountain river.
  • Vest and helmet — always. They must be sound and properly fitted. You don't go on the water without them.
  • Listen to the guide. Commands like "forward", "back", "hold on" must be followed instantly and without debate — on a rapid, seconds count.
  • Don't paddle in flood water. If the water is too high and muddy after heavy rain, a responsible operator will cancel. Insisting on going out is dangerous.
  • Assess yourself honestly. Heart problems, pregnancy, recent injuries — reasons to skip hard routes. Tell the operator in advance.
This article is a general orientation, not a safety manual. The real difficulty and risks depend on the river, weather and water level on the day. Always follow the instructions of your guide and licensed operator on site — they see the actual conditions.

Beginners, families and children

If you've never rafted before — that's fine. Most tourist routes in Georgia are designed exactly for beginners. Before setting off, the guide explains how to hold the paddle, how to paddle on command, what to do if you fall out of the raft — and only then does the group hit the water. Being able to swim helps, but on easy sections the life vest will keep you afloat regardless.

For families with children there are calm grade I–II routes, but limits matter here: operators usually set a minimum age and height for a child and only take children on the quietest rivers in the calm season. Confirm this before booking — a responsible operator won't put a child on a raging rapid.

Beginner's tip: start with an easy route, even if you feel athletic. One short run on a grade-II river will tell you whether you enjoy the sensation and whether to take something tougher next time. Rafting isn't a competition — it's fun.

How a rafting day unfolds

To avoid surprises, it helps to picture how a typical rafting day in Georgia works. It usually looks roughly like this.

Pickup and transfer. In the morning you're collected from your hotel or given a meeting point by the river. The drive to the start is part of the experience: mountain switchbacks, gorges, views. On the way the guide tells you about the river and the order of the day.

Kitting up and briefing. On the bank you're given a wetsuit, neoprene shoes, a life vest and a helmet, and helped to fit it all. Then comes the mandatory briefing: how to hold and paddle, what commands the captain gives, how to sit, what to hold on a rapid and what to do if you go overboard. These are the key 10–15 minutes you can't skip.

The run. The crew boards the raft, takes a few practice strokes on calm water and sets off. From there, fast, rapid-strewn stretches alternate with quiet pools where you can catch your breath, look around and even swim if the guide allows. At the most spectacular rapids a photographer often waits on the bank.

The finish. At the end of the route — out on the bank, change into dry clothes, swap stories and photos. Full-day trips usually include lunch, often with Georgian hospitality, grilled meat and wine. You head home tired, wet and utterly happy.

With the drive, kitting up and lunch, the whole thing takes from half a day to a full day — convenient to build into an itinerary as one item rather than a separate trip.

Other activities near the water

If you enjoy rafting, Georgia has plenty to add to it — the country is generous with active pursuits, and much of it sits in the same regions.

  • Canyoning and canyon walks. In western Georgia, near the rafting rivers, are the famous Martvili and Okatse canyons with boat trips and suspended trails over the water.
  • Zipline and via ferrata. Several gorges have ziplines strung over rivers, and cliffs are rigged with secured climbing routes — adrenaline for those who want more than water.
  • Paragliding. Flights from the mountains over the valleys are a popular add-on to an active trip, especially around Gudauri and the Caucasus range.
  • Jeep tours and hiking. Off-road runs to mountain lakes and hiking trails to waterfalls pair naturally with rafting in one active itinerary.

Many of these can be packed into one full day or spread over a couple of days if you're heading to the mountains or western Georgia specifically for adventure.

Combining rafting with sightseeing

Georgia's big plus is that rafting slots easily into a busy trip without needing a whole holiday of its own. Here are popular pairings:

  • Aragvi + Mtskheta. A run beside the ancient capital plus the Jvari and Svetitskhoveli churches in one day — action and a thousand years of history.
  • Western Georgia + canyons. Rafting on the Rioni or Tekhuri and a boat walk through Martvili Canyon — the perfect "water" day out of Kutaisi.
  • Kura + Borjomi. Rafting, mineral springs and Borjomi park — a mix of adrenaline and relaxation.
  • Active holiday year-round. Rafting in summer, mountain resorts in winter; on winter Georgia, see our guide to Georgia in winter.

Rafting in Georgia FAQ

Do I need experience to go rafting in Georgia?

For basic routes you don't. Beginners are taken on easy-grade rivers (I–III) with a safety briefing, a wetsuit, helmet and life vest, and an experienced guide in the raft. Swimming helps but isn't required on easy sections. Harder grades (IV–V) do need fitness and experience.

When is the best rafting season?

Late spring to early autumn. The biggest water and adrenaline come in May–June during the snowmelt: rivers run full and fast. Summer brings less water and gentler currents — best for beginners and families. Exact dates depend on the river and the year.

How much does rafting in Georgia cost?

It depends on the river, route length and group size. A short easy run is cheaper, a full day on a harder section costs more. The price usually covers gear, a briefing and a guide. Confirm current prices before your trip.

Which rivers are used?

Popular ones are the Aragvi near Mtskheta and the Georgian Military Road, the Kura near Borjomi, the Rioni and Tekhuri in western Georgia, and the mountain rivers of Racha and Adjara. Each has its own difficulty and high-water season.

Is rafting safe for beginners and children?

On easy routes with a proven operator, rafting is a relatively safe family adventure: a life vest, helmet, briefing and experienced guide are mandatory. Children face age limits and only calm sections are used. Always pick a licensed operator and never go out in high flood water.
