# Wine Tours in Georgia: A Local Guide's Honest Take on Kakheti

*By Giorgi Sakhvadze, licensed tour guide based in Tbilisi*

---

Georgia doesn't just make wine. Georgia *invented* wine — or at least that's what every Georgian will tell you within the first five minutes of meeting them. And honestly? The archaeological evidence backs them up. Winemaking here dates back 8,000 years, and the method they still use — burying clay vessels called qvevri underground and fermenting everything together, skins and all — produces a wine unlike anything you've tasted in France or Italy.

I've been guiding wine tours through Kakheti for over seven years. I've watched tourists book glossy "wine experiences" through international platforms and leave disappointed. I've also watched people stumble into a family's backyard and sit down to a five-hour supra feast that they still talk about years later. The difference is almost never about money — it's about knowing what to look for.

Here's my honest breakdown.

## The Qvevri Method: What It Actually Means

Most of the world makes wine by fermenting only the grape juice, separated quickly from skins and seeds. Georgian winemakers doing it the traditional way ferment everything together in a qvevri — a large egg-shaped clay vessel sealed with beeswax and buried in the earth to maintain a stable temperature.

The result is what the natural wine world now calls "amber wine" or "orange wine." The extended skin contact gives the wine a deep amber or copper color, significant tannins (unusual for white wine), and a complex earthy taste. Some people love it immediately. Others need two or three glasses before it clicks. If you've only ever drunk conventional whites, prepare for something genuinely different.

Not every winery in Kakheti works the traditional method. Many use European-style stainless steel tanks to produce more predictable, commercially viable wine. Both approaches are legitimate — just know which one you're booking before you go.

## The Best Wineries to Visit

**Alaverdi Monastery Winery** — This is the one I recommend to almost everyone. The monks at Alaverdi have been making wine here since the 11th century. Their qvevri cellar is stunning, the amber wine is excellent, and the setting inside a functioning monastery complex adds a weight of history that no boutique winery can replicate. Tours are available, but call ahead.

**Pheasant's Tears (Signagi)** — American expat John Wurdeman came to Georgia in the '90s, fell in love with a Georgian artist, and eventually started one of the most respected natural wine operations in the country. The restaurant attached to the winery is exceptional. This is a good option if you want a polished experience without corporate polish.

**Schuchmann Wines** — More upscale, with a hotel and spa on the property. Good if you're combining wine with a comfortable overnight stay in Kakheti. Not the most "authentic" experience, but the wine quality is consistently high and the facilities are excellent.

**Twins Wine House (Napareuli)** — Run by twin brothers who do everything from growing grapes to bottling. The tasting is informal and personal. You'll probably end up at their table eating churchkhela and cheese for two hours. One of my favorites to take guests who want genuine family hospitality.

**Jakeli Winery** — Smaller and less known, but their rkatsiteli amber is one of the best I've tasted. You need to contact them directly to arrange a visit, which keeps the crowds away.

## What to Expect on a Wine Tour

Most organized wine tours from Tbilisi run as day trips, leaving early morning and returning by evening. You'll typically visit two or three wineries, drive through the Alazani Valley, and stop in Sighnaghi — a small hilltop town surrounded by medieval walls that the Georgian government restored about 15 years ago. It looks almost too picturesque, which makes some visitors suspicious, but the setting is genuinely beautiful.

**Prices**: A budget group tour from Tbilisi runs roughly 60–90 GEL per person (about $22–33). A private guided day trip to Kakheti costs 250–400 GEL per person depending on group size and itinerary. For a private tour with a dedicated guide who speaks your language and customizes stops to your interests, expect to pay more — but you'll see things a group tour simply can't fit into its schedule.

## Practical Notes

**When to go**: Late September through early October is harvest season — *rtveli* in Georgian. Wineries are active, grapes are being pressed, and the valley is buzzing. It's also the most crowded time. May and June offer pleasant weather and far fewer tourists. Avoid January through February unless you specifically want a quiet, off-season experience.

**What to eat**: Don't visit a winery without having a proper meal. Churchkhela (walnut-stuffed grape-juice candy), badrijani nigvzit (eggplant with walnut paste), and fresh cheese with honey are standard accompaniments. If a winery invites you to a supra — the Georgian feast tradition with toasts led by a *tamada* — say yes, even if you weren't planning on it.

**Transportation**: You will be drinking. Don't drive yourself unless you have a designated driver. Hire a driver or join a guided tour.

**Don't buy wine at Tbilisi airport**: The prices are inflated and the selection is uninspired. Buy directly from wineries or from Wine Factory No. 1 in Tbilisi before you leave.

Georgia's wine country rewards people who slow down. The best experiences I've seen happen not at the famous wineries but at small family operations that aren't on any top-ten list — where a grandfather who speaks no English still pours you a glass of his homemade chacha and raises a toast to your health in Georgian.

That's what a wine tour here can be, if you approach it right.
