# Why You Should Hire a Local Guide in Tbilisi (And How to Find a Good One)

*By Giorgi Sakhvadze — Tbilisi, Georgia*

---

Tbilisi is easy to navigate independently. The Old Town is compact, most signs in popular areas now include English, and Google Maps works well. You don't *need* a guide to have a good time here.

But here's what I tell people who ask: I've been guiding tourists in this city for seven years, and the visitors who leave knowing they understood what they saw — not just photographed it — are almost always the ones who spent a few hours with someone local. Not because guides have magic information you can't find online. Because context changes everything, and context is what travel blogs can't give you in real time, standing in front of a 6th-century church while the bells are ringing.

Let me break down what guides actually do here, what you should pay, and how to avoid hiring someone who'll disappoint you.

## What a Tbilisi Guide Actually Does

A good guide is not a human audio guide. The best ones do several things:

**They translate culture, not just language.** Why do Georgians toast the way they do? What does the tamada role mean at a supra? Why is that carved wooden balcony in the Old Town called a *darbazi*? Why do some churches face east and others not? These are questions with interesting answers that change how you see everything else.

**They take you to places you wouldn't find or wouldn't enter alone.** A family's courtyard off Abanotubani where the neighborhood women are making churchkhela. A wine shop run by a winemaker who sells only his own production. A view terrace that isn't marked on any map. None of these require a guide, but you won't find them in three days with a phone.

**They negotiate the "local vs tourist price" problem.** In some situations — particularly in the bazaar — having someone who speaks Georgian and clearly knows the area changes what you're offered and what you pay.

**They handle the logistics.** In a city where restaurant reservations don't always get respected and taxis sometimes quote ten times the real rate, having someone next to you who knows how things work removes the low-level friction that exhausts travelers without them realizing it.

## What You Should Pay

Price ranges for Tbilisi guides vary significantly, and the price does not reliably indicate quality.

**Walking tours (group)**: 30–60 GEL per person ($11–22) for a 2–3 hour Old Town walk. These are fine for orientation but the group size often reaches 10–15 people, which limits how much real conversation happens.

**Private walking tour**: 100–200 GEL for 2–4 hours for a private guide. At this level you're getting individual attention and a flexible itinerary. This is the sweet spot for most visitors who want quality without overspending.

**Full private day tour (city + surroundings)**: 300–500 GEL. This covers a car, driver, and guide for a full day — typically combining Tbilisi highlights with Mtskheta or another nearby site. **Red flags for low prices**: Someone offering to guide you for 20 GEL is either unlicensed or supplementing their income by taking commission from restaurants and shops they direct you to. This is common and creates an obvious conflict of interest.

## Red Flags: How to Spot a Bad Guide

I've heard enough tourist horror stories to compile this list honestly.

**They approach you aggressively near Rustaveli Avenue or the Narikala fortress entrance.** Most legitimate guides work by pre-arrangement, not by soliciting strangers. People who approach tourists at major sites are almost always unlicensed.

**They can't answer basic historical questions without obvious uncertainty.** Ask something specific: when was Narikala fortress built, and by whom? What's the difference between the Sioni Cathedral and Metekhi Church architecturally? A good guide has confident, detailed answers. A bad one says "interesting question" and pivots.

**They steer every dining decision toward one specific restaurant.** Georgian food is genuinely wonderful everywhere. If your guide insists you eat at one particular place, they're almost certainly collecting referral fees there.

**They don't ask you anything about your interests.** A guide who doesn't ask what you care about — history, architecture, food, religion, modern Tbilisi, Soviet history — is running a fixed script, not a customized experience.

**Their license is vague or nonexistent.** Georgia introduced licensed guide certification through the Georgian National Tourism Administration. It's not a guarantee of quality, but unlicensed guides operating outside the system have no accountability if something goes wrong.

## How to Find a Good One

**Book through a legitimate agency or platform, not random street encounters.** Look for agencies that show specific guide profiles with languages, specializations, and real reviews — not just generic five-star ratings.

**Ask for the guide's name before you book.** If an agency won't commit to which specific guide you'll get, move on. The variation in quality between individual guides at the same agency can be enormous.

**Check reviews on Google Maps, TripAdvisor, and Booking.com** — but read the text, not just the stars. Look for reviews that describe specific things the guide knew or did, not just "great experience, highly recommend."

**Match the guide's languages to your needs.** For Russian-speaking visitors, a guide fluent in Russian (not just basic conversational) makes a significant difference. The same goes for German, French, or Hebrew speakers — Georgia has significant tourism from all of these markets, and specialists exist.

**Ask what the tour includes upfront.** Admission fees to Narikala, the Jvari Monastery, or other sites add up. Know whether these are included or separate before you go.

## What You'll Actually Get

Tbilisi has been called the city where East meets West so many times it's become a cliché — but it's a cliché because it's true, and because the layers are real. Persian, Mongol, Ottoman, Russian, Soviet, and now independent Georgia have all left marks on the same streets, sometimes on the same building. A good guide helps you read those layers.

The Old Town's Abanotubani sulfur baths district, the medieval Metekhi Church perched above the Mtkvari river, Rustaveli Avenue with its mix of Soviet neoclassical architecture and 21st-century shopping, Fabrika — the repurposed Soviet sewing factory that's now the city's hippest hangout — each of these makes more sense when you know the thread running through them.

You don't need a guide in Tbilisi. But if you're only here for three or four days, a good one will give you a year's worth of context in a morning.
